How to read your market brief

The brief tells you what the market is likely to care about today; it does not tell you what to buy. Use it to decide what to watch, what to avoid, and how much risk the day is likely to carry.

All times are Central. General education, not personal investment advice.

A 10-minute morning routine

Read the brief top to bottom, broad to narrow: first decide what kind of day it is, then what is moving, then whether any of it fits your rules.

  1. Read the headline and "What's driving today." Ask one question: is today about the whole market (a Fed speaker, an inflation number, a bond selloff) or about individual companies (earnings, deals)?
  2. Scan the snapshot. Are futures up or down, and by how much? Is the VIX rising? Is the 10-year yield climbing? This sets the day's mood.
  3. Check the calendar. Note any release at 7:30 a.m. or 9:00 a.m. Prices often jump around those times.
  4. Look at the Gap watchlist and Movers. These are the stocks with news. Read the catalyst column before the % column.
  5. Check your own holdings. Is anything you own, or its underlying, in the movers, earnings or leveraged ETF tables?
  6. Decide what you will not do today. Most good decisions on a busy morning are things you skip.

Movers and the Gap watchlist

A big percentage move matters only if you know why it happened and whether real money is behind it. Read three columns together: catalyst, relative volume, % change.

  • Catalyst. A buyout at a fixed price (PTC, RXO on Oct 5) usually stalls near the deal price, so most of the move is already done. Earnings, guidance or trial data can keep moving for days. "No confirmed catalyst" means nobody knows why the stock moved, so treat that move with suspicion.
  • Relative volume. Today's volume divided by the stock's normal volume. 1× is an ordinary day; 3× or more means many more traders than usual are involved. A big move on low relative volume can reverse easily.
  • % change. A gap is the jump between yesterday's close and today's first price. Gaps on strong news with heavy volume can keep going. Gaps on weak or no news often partly reverse during the day ("gap fill" or "fade").

The Gap watchlist applies these checks for you: only $5+ stocks worth $2B+, gapping 3% or more, with confirmed news. It is a list of names worth reading about, not a list to buy. A gap-down on a downgrade is on the list for the same reason as a gap-up on earnings: both are where the action is.

Two patterns to recognise: follow-the-leader (one country, sector or theme moving together, like the Brazilian stocks on Oct 5 after the election) and news then dilution (a biotech jumps on trial data, then sells new shares after the close, as Vaxcyte did). Check the risk note before reading anything into a big gain.

The pattern to recognise is the round trip: SDEV rose 104% on Oct 2 and fell 47% on Oct 5. Two big up days in a row with no news (QTEX, SCKT) often look like the first half of that pattern.

Leveraged ETFs

A leveraged ETF multiplies the underlying's move for one day only, then resets. Over one day TQQQ should move about 3× the Nasdaq-100. Over several days the result drifts from 3×, and in choppy markets it loses money even when the index ends flat.

Two days where the index rises 10% and then falls 10%, starting from $100:

HoldingAfter day 1After day 2Net
Index (1×)$110$99−1%
3× bull (like TQQQ)$130$91−9%
3× bear (like SQQQ)$70$91−9%

The index lost 1% while both the bull and the bear funds lost 9%. This is called volatility decay. It is why these funds are built for short holds and why holding them for weeks needs care.

  • Look at the underlying column first. TSLL is a bet on Tesla, SOXL on chip stocks, NVDL on Nvidia. The driver notes say what moved each one.
  • Bull and bear pairs mirror each other (TQQQ/SQQQ, SOXL/SOXS, TSLL/TSLQ, SPXL/SPXU, TNA/TZA). If one is up, its pair is down by roughly the same multiple.
  • Size accordingly. A 3× fund moves like three positions in the index. A 3% index drop is roughly a 9% drop in TQQQ.
  • n/a rows mean the brief couldn't get a fresh price, so check your broker for the live quote.

Earnings and the calendar

Much of a day's volatility is scheduled, and the brief tells you when.

Time (CT)What happensWhy it matters
3:00–8:30 a.m.Pre-market tradingThin volume; prices can swing and spreads are wide.
7:30 a.m.Most big economic releases (jobs, inflation, trade)Futures can jump in seconds.
8:30 a.m.Market opensThe first 15–30 minutes are usually the most volatile of the day.
9:00 a.m.Second wave of data (consumer sentiment, housing)Another possible jolt.
1:00 p.m.Fed decisions (on meeting days)Can move the whole market sharply.
3:00 p.m.Market closes; after-hours beginsAfter-close earnings land from here on.

For earnings: before the open means the results are already out and the stock's pre-market move shows the first reaction. After the close means the reaction comes tonight, and you can't trade out at today's close price once the news hits. A Fed speaker or a fresh inflation number can override everything else in the brief, so check the calendar before acting on any single stock.

Ground rules for turning the brief into a decision

These are widely used beginner guardrails, not personal advice. Check anything with real money at stake against your own situation.

  • Decide your maximum loss before you enter. Many traders cap the risk on any one trade at 1–2% of their account, and set the exit point before buying.
  • Don't chase the open. Waiting 15–30 minutes after 8:30 a.m. lets the first wild swings settle and shows whether a gap is holding or fading.
  • No catalyst, no trade. If you can't say in one sentence why a stock is moving, you can't judge whether the move will last.
  • Respect the market's mood. Buying a strong stock is harder on a day the snapshot is red and the VIX is jumping.
  • Use limit orders before the open and on thin stocks. A limit order sets your price; a market order takes whatever is available, which can be far off in pre-market.
  • Be careful with leverage and options. Losses compound faster; know how a position behaves if you're wrong for three days.
  • Keep a short journal. Write what you did, why, and what the brief said. Reviewing it after a few weeks teaches you more than any single day.

Glossary

TermMeaning
FuturesContracts that trade nearly around the clock and show where indexes are heading before the open.
Pre-marketTrading before the 8:30 a.m. CT open; thinner and jumpier than the regular session.
GapThe difference between yesterday's close and today's opening price.
Gap fill / fadeA gap that partly or fully reverses during the day.
CatalystThe news behind a move: earnings, a deal, an upgrade, trial data.
Relative volumeToday's volume divided by normal volume; 3× means three times the usual trading.
Dollar volumeShares traded × price; shows where the most money is moving.
Market capShare price × shares outstanding; the company's total stock value.
FloatShares available to trade freely; a small float moves more easily.
Upgrade / downgradeAn analyst raising or lowering their rating on a stock.
GuidanceA company's own forecast for coming quarters; often moves a stock more than the results.
BMO / AMCBefore market open / after market close: when a company reports earnings.
EPS estimateWhat analysts expect a company to earn per share; results are judged against it.
Dilution / offeringA company selling new shares, which shrinks existing holders' slice.
Reverse splitMerging shares (say 10 into 1) to raise a low share price.
Leverage (2×, 3×)A fund aiming to move two or three times the underlying's daily change.
Bull / bear fundA bull fund rises when its underlying rises; a bear fund rises when it falls.
Basis point (bp)One hundredth of a percent: a yield moving from 5.26% to 5.31% rose 5 bp.
Limit orderAn order that only fills at your price or better.
